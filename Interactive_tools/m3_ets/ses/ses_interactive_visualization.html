This tool walks through simple exponential smoothing one step at a time on monthly airline-passenger data, where each forecast is nothing more than a weighted blend of the last observation and the prior level. Drag the smoothing parameter or the starting level to watch the fitted line, the level path, and the training and test error recompute in real time.

⭐ What does "Find the Best Fit" do?

It asks one question: which value of α make the one-step-ahead forecasts fit the training months best? For every candidate it runs the same recursion you watch in the animation across the 115 training months, adds up the squared errors between each forecast ŷt and the actual yt, and keeps the candidate with the smallest total (the SSE). That is exactly the criterion statsmodels and R's ets() use when errors are additive, so the answer you get here is the answer you would get in Python or R.

  • Starting values. By default the initial level ℓ0 is estimated as well, not just the smoothing parameters. This needs no extra searching because the forecasts depend linearly on the starting values, so for any candidate α the best starting values are an ordinary least-squares solution. Tick "keep my starting values fixed" to search only over the smoothing parameters and keep the sliders where you set them.
  • Allowed region. The search uses the same parameter region as statsmodels: 0 ≤ α ≤ 1. With α = 1 the level is simply the last observation and the forecast becomes the naive "tomorrow = today" rule; with α = 0 the level never moves. Results are snapped to the sliders' 0.01 grid.
  • Training only. The test months play no part in the search. A lower training RMSE does not guarantee a lower test RMSE, and comparing the two after clicking the button is the point of the exercise.

What to look for. On the airline data the best training fit is α = 1. That is the naive forecast, and it is a sign that a level-only model is missing the trend and the seasonality rather than a recommendation to use it. Compare its test RMSE with a hand-picked α such as 0.3, then move on to Holt and Holt-Winters, where the optimizer has something better to find.

📚 Parameter Guide: Understanding Alpha (α)

🎯 The Alpha Parameter: The Heart of Simple Exponential Smoothing

🔻 Low Alpha (α ≈ 0.1 - 0.3)

Smooth & Stable: Creates very smooth forecasts that change slowly. The model gives more weight to historical data, making it resistant to noise but slow to adapt to genuine changes.

🔺 High Alpha (α ≈ 0.7 - 0.9)

Responsive & Reactive: Quickly adapts to recent changes in the data. The model prioritizes recent observations, making forecasts more volatile but responsive to new patterns.

⚠️ When Alpha Approaches 1.0: A Warning Signal

When optimal α is very high (>0.95), the model is essentially saying "I can only trust the most recent observation." This typically indicates that Simple Exponential Smoothing is inadequate for your data, likely because the data contains trend or seasonal patterns that SES cannot capture. Time to consider Holt's or Holt-Winters methods!

💡 Mathematical Intuition

The α parameter controls the exponential decay of historical weights. With α = 0.3, the current observation gets 30% weight, the previous gets 21% (0.7 × 0.3), the one before gets 14.7% (0.7² × 0.3), and so on. This creates the characteristic "exponential smoothing" effect where influence decreases exponentially with age.
