🎯 Forecast Equation

ŷt+h|t = ℓt + h·bt
Forecast = Level + (Horizon × Trend)

📊 Level Equation

ℓt = α·yt + (1-α)·(ℓt-1 + bt-1)
Smooths between observation and one-step forecast

📈 Trend Equation

bt = β*·(ℓt - ℓt-1) + (1-β*)·bt-1
Smooths the trend estimate

🔍 Holt vs SES: The Key Difference

SES: Assumes data fluctuates around a constant level → Flat forecasts

Holt: Adds a trend component that captures direction → Sloped forecasts that can follow upward/downward patterns!

⭐ What does "Find the Best Fit" do?

It asks one question: which values of α and β* make the one-step-ahead forecasts fit the training months best? For every candidate it runs the same recursion you watch in the animation across the 115 training months, adds up the squared errors between each forecast ŷt and the actual yt, and keeps the candidate with the smallest total (the SSE). That is exactly the criterion statsmodels and R's ets() use when errors are additive, and the search below keeps to statsmodels' default parameter region, so the answer you get here is the answer you would get in Python.

  • Starting values. By default the initial level ℓ0 and initial trend b0 are estimated as well, not just the smoothing parameters. This needs no extra searching because the forecasts depend linearly on the starting values, so for any candidate α and β* the best starting values are an ordinary least-squares solution. Tick "keep my starting values fixed" to search only over the smoothing parameters and keep the sliders where you set them.
  • Allowed region. The search uses the default parameter region of statsmodels: 0 ≤ α ≤ 1 and 0 ≤ β* ≤ α. The cap on β* is a statsmodels default rather than a textbook rule (Hyndman's usual region only asks for 0 ≤ β* ≤ 1). On the airline data it makes no difference: the best fit has β* = 0 either way. Results are snapped to the sliders' 0.01 grid.
  • Training only. The test months play no part in the search. A lower training RMSE does not guarantee a lower test RMSE, and comparing the two after clicking the button is the point of the exercise.

What to look for. On the airline data the best training fit is α = 1 and β* = 0: the level is the last observation and the trend is frozen at its estimated starting slope. The result extrapolates a straight line through the seasonal swings, and its test RMSE is far worse than the default settings. Answers that sit on the edge of the allowed region are common and worth a discussion: they mean the data are pushing against the model's assumptions, here the missing seasonality.

📚 Parameter Guide: Understanding Alpha (α) and Beta* (β*)

🎯 Dual Parameter Control: Level and Trend Components

📊 Alpha (α) - Level Smoothing

Controls level responsiveness:

  • Low α (0.1-0.3): Stable level, slow to adapt
  • High α (0.7-0.9): Responsive level, quick adaptation

📈 Beta* (β*) - Trend Smoothing

Controls trend responsiveness:

  • Low β* (0.1-0.3): Stable trend, resistant to noise
  • High β* (0.7-0.9): Adaptive trend, follows changes

⚡ High α, Low β*

Level adapts quickly to changes, but trend remains stable. Good for data with level shifts but consistent trend direction.

🔄 Low α, High β* (outside the optimizer's region)

Find Best Fit only searches β* ≤ α, the statsmodels default. With a small α and a large β* the trend can swing by tens of passengers a month while the level barely moves. The sliders will let you try it. The optimizer will not.

🎯 Forecast Impact: ŷt+h|t = ℓt + h·bt

The forecast combines both components: the current level plus the trend multiplied by the forecast horizon. Longer horizons amplify the trend effect, making β* increasingly important for long-term forecasts. A stable trend (low β*) produces consistent long-term forecasts, while an adaptive trend (high β*) can lead to dramatic fan-out effects.

🔬 Component Independence

Unlike SES where only α matters, Holt's method allows independent control over how quickly the model adapts its understanding of "where we are" (level) versus "where we're going" (trend). This dual-parameter approach enables much more nuanced modeling of trending time series patterns.
